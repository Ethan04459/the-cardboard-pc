Where it all began

Introduction

Have you ever wondered what's actually inside a computer? Most people use computers every day — but very few have ever seen the components that make them work. We wanted to change that. Our project, Cardboard PC, set out to build a full-scale physical model of a desktop computer using nothing but cardboard and craft materials.

Our goal was simple: make the inside of a PC accessible, understandable, and tangible for everyone — no technical background needed. By recreating each component by hand, we wanted people to be able to look at our model and immediately understand what each part does, how it connects to the rest of the system, and why it matters.

This website is an extension of that model — a digital companion that lets you explore every component in detail, see how they apply to real-world tasks, and understand why the hardware inside a computer is so important.

How we built it

Build Process & Methodology

From blank cardboard to a fully labelled PC model — here's every step we took.

01

We began by researching the actual size of a standard PC case and its internal components. Using these measurements, we created a scaled diagram to ensure our cardboard model would be realistic and correctly proportioned.

02

Next, we studied what each component looks like in real life — including the CPU, GPU, RAM, PSU, SSDs, motherboard, and connectors. We collected reference images to accurately recreate their shapes and layouts.

03

We learned where each part is located inside an actual PC — including motherboard slots, power connectors, airflow paths, and how components interact. We then mapped these positions onto our diagram.

04

Using all our research, we produced a final top-down diagram showing the exact placement of every component. This served as our blueprint for the cardboard build.

05

Finally, we cut and assembled each component out of cardboard, following the diagram for accuracy. We layered pieces to add depth, labelled each part, and arranged everything to match a real PC's internal structure.

The bigger picture

How Components Work Together

Every component has a job — but the magic happens when they all work as one system.

⚡
System Boot: Press Power → OS Loads
What actually happens in those first few seconds
1
Power Button Pressed

You press the power button. A signal travels to the PSU, which converts mains AC electricity into the stable DC voltages the computer needs (+12V, +5V, +3.3V).

↓
2
Power Good Signal

The PSU sends a "Power Good" signal to the Motherboard, confirming voltages are stable. The VRM then steps voltage down further to the precise level the CPU needs.

↓
3
CPU Wakes Up

The CPU receives power and begins executing its first instruction — a hardcoded address pointing to the BIOS/UEFI firmware stored on the motherboard chip.

↓
4
POST — Power-On Self Test

The BIOS runs a POST, checking that RAM is present and working, storage devices are connected (SSD), and the GPU is responding. If anything fails, it beeps an error code.

↓
5
Bootloader Found

The BIOS finds the boot device (your SSD) and loads the bootloader into RAM. The bootloader hands control over to the operating system kernel.

↓
6
OS Loads — You're In

The OS kernel is copied from the SSD into RAM. The CPU starts running OS processes, the GPU renders the login screen, and the Case Fans spin up to manage heat. You see the desktop.

Under the hood

The Science Behind It

Why are components designed the way they are? Here's the physics and engineering behind each one.

⚡
VRMs
Voltage Conversion
tap to explore
How VRMs Convert 12V → ~1V

The PSU delivers 12V but CPUs need only ~1V. VRMs use buck converter circuits — rapidly switching transistors (MOSFETs) on and off at millions of times per second. Each switch cycle transfers a tiny packet of energy, and by controlling the duty cycle (how long the switch is on vs off), the output voltage is precisely regulated. Inductors and capacitors smooth the pulses into clean, stable DC power.

🎮
GPUs
Thousands of Cores
tap to explore
Why GPUs Have Thousands of Small Cores

Rendering graphics means processing millions of pixels simultaneously — each needing the same maths applied independently. GPUs exploit this with massive parallelism: instead of a few powerful cores (like a CPU), they use thousands of simpler cores running the same instruction on many data points at once (SIMD). This is perfect for matrix maths, shader calculations, and AI — tasks that are embarrassingly parallel by nature.

💾
SSDs
NAND Flash
tap to explore
Why SSDs Use NAND Flash

NAND flash stores data as trapped electrons inside floating-gate transistors. Applying voltage forces electrons through an oxide layer into the gate, where they remain even without power — this is non-volatile storage. NAND is called "flash" because erasing an entire block of cells simultaneously (like a camera flash) is far faster than erasing one cell at a time. No moving parts means microsecond access times vs milliseconds for HDDs.

🧠
RAM
Why It's Volatile
tap to explore
Why RAM Loses Data When Power Cuts

DRAM stores each bit as a tiny electric charge in a capacitor. Capacitors leak charge naturally, so they must be refreshed thousands of times per second — requiring constant power. The moment power is cut, the capacitors discharge and all data is lost. This volatility is a trade-off: capacitor-based cells are incredibly fast and dense, making RAM thousands of times quicker than flash storage.

🗂️
Motherboard
Circuits & Traces
tap to explore
Why Motherboards Use Copper Traces

A motherboard is a printed circuit board (PCB) — layers of fibreglass with copper pathways (traces) etched between them. Copper is used because it has very low electrical resistance. The traces are incredibly thin (sometimes under 0.1mm) and precisely routed to minimise signal interference and capacitance. Modern motherboards have up to 12 layers of copper, allowing thousands of signals to travel simultaneously without crossing.

🔌
PCIe
Lanes & Speed
tap to explore
Why PCIe Lanes Matter for Speed

Each PCIe lane is a pair of wires that sends data in both directions simultaneously (full-duplex). PCIe 4.0 transfers ~2 GB/s per lane. A GPU uses x16 lanes — 16 × 2 GB/s = up to 32 GB/s of bandwidth. More lanes = more data in parallel, like widening a motorway from 1 to 16 lanes. Limiting a GPU to x4 lanes bottlenecks performance because the GPU's frame data can't reach the CPU fast enough.

Heat & cooling

Thermal Science

Heat is the enemy of performance. Here's why PCs get hot and how they fight it.

🔥
Why PCs Overheat

Every electrical component wastes some energy as heat (P = I²R — power lost = current squared × resistance). At high clock speeds, billions of transistors switch every second, each dissipating a tiny amount of heat. Combined, a CPU or GPU can generate over 200W of heat in a space smaller than your palm.

🌡️
Why GPUs Run Hotter Than CPUs

GPUs have far more transistors (over 80 billion on modern cards) all active simultaneously during rendering. A CPU might use 8–16 cores; a GPU uses thousands at once. More simultaneous activity = more heat generated per second. GPU TDPs (thermal design power) regularly exceed 300–450W vs a CPU's 65–125W.

⚠️
What Thermal Throttling Is

When a CPU or GPU exceeds its safe temperature limit (~95–105°C), it automatically reduces its clock speed to generate less heat. This is thermal throttling — the chip sacrifices performance to prevent physical damage. You'll notice it as sudden FPS drops or stuttering. Good cooling prevents throttling entirely.

📐
Why Heatsinks Use Fins

Heat transfers from a surface to air proportionally to surface area. A flat block of aluminium has limited surface area. By cutting it into dozens of thin fins, the same volume of metal has 10–20× more surface touching the air, so heat escapes far faster. The fins are also spaced to encourage airflow between them without too much resistance.

🌀
Why Fans Need Static Pressure

Airflow fans are measured in CFM (cubic feet per minute) and static pressure (mmH₂O). Static pressure describes a fan's ability to push air through resistance — like a dense heatsink or radiator. A high-CFM fan with low static pressure stalls when it meets resistance. Heatsink and radiator fans need high static pressure; case fans moving open air need high CFM.

What we achieved

Conclusion

Through the Cardboard PC project, we successfully built a complete, labelled model of a desktop computer — and in doing so, proved that understanding technology doesn't require expensive equipment or a classroom. It just takes curiosity and a willingness to build.

Every component we recreated — from the CPU to the case fans — helped us understand not just what each part does, but why it matters and how it fits into the bigger picture of a working computer. The process of building it forced us to research, collaborate, and explain ideas clearly, which deepened our own understanding along the way.

We hope that anyone who interacts with our project — whether at the STEM Fair or on this website — walks away with a clearer picture of what's inside the machine they use every day. If we've made computers even a little less mysterious, then we've done our job.
